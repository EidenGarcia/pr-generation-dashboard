What a kWh costs you

Your bill has two kinds of charges. The cost of generating the power (fuel, and energy bought from private producers) changes by the hour with which plants are running; on the bill it is averaged and updated every three months as the FCA and PPCA charges. The rest (the base rate that pays for operating the system, subsidies and municipal contributions) is fixed until the Energy Bureau revises it. This section estimates the generation part from the live cost of the plants and adds the fixed part approved for the current quarter.

Ramp rates

How fast generation has to rise or fall to follow demand, measured as the change over 60 minutes (MW per hour). Steep ramps, usually in the afternoon and evening, must be met by plants that can change output quickly. The older steam plants ramp slowly, so fast ramps often fall to the diesel turbines, which cost more.

Steepest rise and drop each day

Each day’s largest increase (up) and decrease (down) in generation over any 60 minutes.

Daily demand range

Frequency against IEEE limits

The grid runs at 60 Hz. The further frequency strays and the longer it stays away, the closer generators and inverters get to tripping off. Each 5-minute reading from PREPA is placed in a level below; any reading outside Normal starts an excursion, logged with its duration and whether it outlasted the time allowed at that level.

Last 24 hours on the limit scale

The scale stretches the area near 60 Hz so small swings stay visible while the IEEE limits remain on the chart. Dashed lines are IEEE 1547-2018 trip settings.

    How the island’s generation is dispatched

    Live analysis from every saved reading: which plants are cheapest, which ones carry the load, what a cheapest-first dispatch would change, and how much of the demand is constant.

    Merit order: the island’s supply curve

    Each block is a plant: its width is how much it can supply, its height what each MWh costs. Renewables producing now are taken first; the other plants follow from cheapest to most expensive. Where the demand line crosses the curve is the plant that should set the cost. Plants to the right of the line that are running anyway are “out of merit”.

    Who is carrying the load

    The share of generation coming from baseload plants (run all day), peaking plants (started for the highest demand, usually costly diesel) and renewables. A growing peaking share means more expensive power.

    What would change right now

    If the same demand were met using the cheapest running plants first, which plants would produce more and which less. Long bars mean the current dispatch is far from cheapest-first.

    Load duration curve, last 24 hours

    Every 5-minute reading sorted from highest to lowest. The lower block is base load, needed all the time; the band above it comes and goes during the day and must be met by plants that can follow load.
