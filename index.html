About this dashboard

Puerto Rico's generation fleet is a mix of older steam plants, combined-cycle units, diesel peakers, independent producers and renewables. Which ones run at any hour decides both what customers pay and how much margin the grid has if a unit trips.

This site brings that information together in one place. Each section below covers one topic and starts with a short explanation. New to the terms? The Glossary explains them, and About covers data sources and methods.

      What a kWh costs you

      Your bill has two kinds of charges. The cost of generating the power (fuel, and energy bought from private producers) changes by the hour with which plants are running; on the bill it is averaged and updated every three months as the FCA and PPCA charges. The rest (the base rate that pays for operating the system, subsidies and municipal contributions) is fixed until the Energy Bureau revises it. This section estimates the generation part from the live cost of the plants and adds the fixed part approved for the current quarter.

      Generation over time

      The whole generation: every plant’s output added up, by plant or by fuel. Pick today (from 12 AM), the last full day, any date, or the last 7 or 30 days. The dashed line is demand, PREPA’s own figure. The right axis shows the ramp of whatever plants are shown: hide plants in the legend to see one plant’s ramp. Drag to zoom.

      Reactive power (MVAr) and power factor

      How the reactive power each plant supplies changes through the day, and the power factor that results, for the whole system or for one plant.

      Ramp rates

      How fast generation has to rise or fall to follow demand, measured as the change over 60 minutes (MW per hour). Steep ramps, usually in the afternoon and evening, must be met by plants that can change output quickly. The older steam plants ramp slowly, so fast ramps often fall to the diesel turbines, which cost more.

      Steepest rise and drop each day

      Each day’s largest increase (up) and decrease (down) in generation over any 60 minutes.

      Daily demand range

      Each day’s lowest and highest demand. The taller the bar, the more generation had to be brought on and taken off during the day.

      How fast each plant ramps

      Measured from consecutive readings (every 15 minutes) while the plant was online, so start-ups and trips are not counted here. Typical is the median rate when the plant was ramping. Fast plants can follow quick changes in demand; slow ones cannot.

      Reserve adequacy: could the system lose its largest unit?

      Reserve is generation held ready to replace a unit that trips. The minimum required here follows an N-1 rule: there must be enough reserve to cover losing the largest unit online at that moment (N-1-1: the two largest). When the reserve is below that, losing that unit could mean load shedding.

      Grid frequency

      The grid runs at 60 Hz, and how far and how long it strays shows how stressed it is. Every 5-minute reading is measured against IEEE 1547-2018 limits, with statistics and a log of each excursion.

      On this pageLast 24 hours and levelsExcursion statisticsExcursion log

      Frequency against IEEE limits

      The grid runs at 60 Hz. The further frequency strays and the longer it stays away, the closer generators and inverters get to tripping off. Each 5-minute reading from PREPA is placed in a level below; any reading outside Normal starts an excursion, logged with its duration and whether it outlasted the time allowed at that level.

      Last 24 hours on the limit scale

      The scale stretches the area near 60 Hz so small swings stay visible while the IEEE limits remain on the chart. Dashed lines are IEEE 1547-2018 trip settings.

      Levels and where they come from

        Readings are 5 minutes apart, so short dips between readings can be missed and durations are estimates. “Possibly exceeded” means a single reading was outside the range and the event could have lasted longer than allowed.

        Excursion statistics

        How often frequency leaves the Normal range, whether that is getting more or less frequent, and when it happens: at the evening peak, and when the operating reserve is low.

        How the island’s generation is dispatched

        Live analysis from every saved reading: which plants are cheapest, which ones carry the load, what a cheapest-first dispatch would change, and how much of the demand is constant.

        Merit order: the island’s supply curve

        Each block is a plant: its width is how much it can supply, its height what each MWh costs. Renewables producing now are taken first; the other plants follow from cheapest to most expensive. Where the demand line crosses the curve is the plant that should set the cost. Plants to the right of the line that are running anyway are “out of merit”.

        Who is carrying the load

        The share of generation coming from baseload plants (run all day), peaking plants (started for the highest demand, usually costly diesel) and renewables. A growing peaking share means more expensive power.

        Load duration curve, last 24 hours

        Every 5-minute reading sorted from highest to lowest. The lower block is base load, needed all the time; the band above it comes and goes during the day and must be met by plants that can follow load.

        Glossary

        Plain-language definitions of the terms used across this site, from megawatts and reserves to the charges on your bill. Type in the search box to find a term.

        On this pagePower and energyRunning the gridPlants and dispatchOrganizationsYour billFrequency limits

        No terms match that search.

        Power and energy

        MW (megawatt)
        A rate of power: how much electricity is being produced or used at a given moment. 1 MW = 1,000 kW.
        MWh / kWh
        An amount of energy: power sustained over time. 1 MW for one hour is 1 MWh. Your bill is measured in kWh (1 MWh = 1,000 kWh).
        MVAr (reactive power)
        Power that does no useful work but is needed to keep voltage up so real power can flow through the lines. Generators supply it alongside their MW.
        Power factor
        The share of total power that is real power (MW). 1.0 is ideal; lower values mean more reactive power is flowing for the same useful output.
        Frequency (Hz)
        How many times per second the alternating current cycles. Puerto Rico's grid runs at 60 Hz. It drops when demand exceeds generation and rises when generation exceeds demand.

        Running the grid

        Demand (load)
        How much electricity customers are using at a moment. On this site it is PREPA's own system total.
        Generation
        The sum of the output of every generating unit. It runs a little above demand because some power is lost in the lines.
        Available capacity
        The output the units that are online or ready could deliver right now.
        Capacity margin
        Available capacity minus demand: how much room is left before the system cannot meet load.
        Spinning reserve
        Extra output from units already running and synchronized, available within seconds to minutes if another unit trips.
        Operating reserve
        Spinning reserve plus units that can be started and loaded within about an hour.
        N-1 / N-1-1
        Reliability rules. N-1: the system must survive losing its single largest unit online. N-1-1: it must survive losing the two largest.
        Load shedding
        Deliberately cutting power to some customers to keep the rest of the grid from collapsing when supply cannot meet demand.
        Ramp rate
        How fast output changes, in MW per hour. Demand rises steeply in the afternoon and evening, and plants must ramp to follow it.
        Load duration curve
        Every reading in a period sorted from highest to lowest demand. It shows how much load is always present (base) and how much comes and goes.

        Plants and dispatch

        Baseload plant
        A plant run most hours of the day, usually large steam or combined-cycle units that are slow to start and stop.
        Peaker
        A plant started for the highest demand, usually fast diesel combustion turbines that cost more per kWh.
        Combined cycle
        A plant that burns fuel in a gas turbine and reuses its hot exhaust to make steam for a second turbine, getting more power from the same fuel.
        Steam unit
        A unit that burns fuel (such as Bunker C) in a boiler to drive a steam turbine. Most of the island's older large units are of this type.
        Bunker C
        A heavy residual fuel oil burned in older steam units.
        Dispatch
        Deciding which units run and how much each produces at each moment.
        Dispatch cost
        Each unit's output multiplied by its reported cost per MWh, added up across units ($ per hour).
        Merit order
        Units ranked from cheapest to most expensive to run. Following it means using the cheapest available power first.
        Out of merit
        A unit running even though cheaper units have room, for example for voltage support, reliability or because it cannot be shut down quickly.

        Organizations

        PREPA
        Puerto Rico Electric Power Authority (AEE). It owns the public generation fleet and publishes the operational data used on this site.
        Genera PR
        The private company that operates PREPA's legacy power plants.
        LUMA
        The private company that operates the transmission and distribution system, including announcing load relief.
        Independent producers (PPOA)
        Privately owned plants that sell power to PREPA under power purchase and operating agreements, such as the gas, coal and renewable plants.
        Energy Bureau (PREB / NEPR)
        The Puerto Rico Energy Bureau, the regulator that approves rates and quarterly fuel and purchased-power charges.

        Your bill

        FCA
        Fuel Charge Adjustment: passes through the cost of fuel for Genera's plants. Set every quarter.
        PPCA
        Purchased Power Charge Adjustment: passes through the cost of energy bought from independent producers. Set every quarter.
        Base rate
        The part of the price that pays for operating and maintaining the system. Fixed until the Energy Bureau revises it.
        CELI
        Contribution in lieu of taxes: the charge that covers electricity supplied to municipalities.
        Subsidies
        Charges that fund discounted rates for eligible groups and public uses.

        Frequency limits

        Excursion
        A period when frequency leaves the Normal range. Each one is logged with its duration and most extreme value.
        Normal
        59.8 to 60.2 Hz. This dashboard's setting for normal regulation.
        Watch
        59.4 to 59.8 Hz or 60.2 to 60.6 Hz. Within the limits conventional generators can operate in continuously.
        Alert
        58.8 to 59.4 Hz or 60.6 to 61.2 Hz. Inside IEEE 1547-2018 continuous operation, but time-limited for generators (about 3 minutes).
        Critical
        Below 58.8 Hz or above 61.2 Hz. Outside IEEE 1547-2018 continuous operation; ride-through for up to 299 seconds, then trip settings apply.
        IEEE 1547-2018
        The US standard for connecting generators and inverters to the grid, including the frequencies at which they must stay online or trip.

        About this site

        What this dashboard is for, where its data comes from, how it is updated and saved, and the limits of what it can show.

        On this pagePurposeData sourcesUpdates and saved dataLimitations

        Purpose

        Puerto Rico's electricity is among the most expensive and least reliable in the United States, yet the information that explains why is scattered across operational feeds, regulatory filings and reports. This site gathers it in one place and keeps it updated, so residents, journalists, researchers and decision makers can see what the grid is doing and what it costs.

        Where the data comes from

        • PREPA operational data portal: dataSource.js (plants, units, metrics) and dataGraph.js (24-hour generation and frequency at 5-minute intervals).
        • Energy Bureau (PREB): approved base rate, FCA and PPCA factors and quarterly filings, including Genera's filings in docket NEPR-MI-2019-0007 used to rank units.
        • Transmission map: Energy Bureau filing NEPR-MI-2024-0005, the 2023 PREPA Fiscal Plan and LUMA reports, as cited on the Plants page.
        • Frequency limits: IEEE 1547-2018 and generator operating practice in North America.

        How it is updated and saved

        • In your browser: while the page is open it reads PREPA's feed every 5 minutes and stores every reading in this device's own database, so charts keep growing between visits. Download history (CSV) exports it. Moving between tabs or reloading never deletes it.
        • On GitHub: an automated job checks the feeds every 15 minutes, day and night, and commits the shared history, unit event logs, daily summaries and portal health to the repository, so every visitor sees the same record.

        Methods and limitations

        • Readings are 5 to 15 minutes apart, so short events between readings can be missed and durations are estimates.
        • A unit producing under 1 MW counts as offline. The feed does not say whether a unit is on forced outage, maintenance or reserve.
        • The 24-hour forecast and the price-per-kWh estimate are this site's own calculations, not official figures.
        • Costs use each unit's reported cost; units without a reported cost are left out of cost totals.

        Disclaimer

        This is an independent project. It is not an official product of PREPA, Genera PR, LUMA or the Energy Bureau. Supply warnings here are early indications from public data; LUMA announces actual load relief.
